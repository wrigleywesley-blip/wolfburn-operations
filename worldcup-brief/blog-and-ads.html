Dispatch · The Wolfburn Journal

What Archie Gemmill knew.

On Wembley, 1967. On Mendoza, 1978. On a wooden box, 2026. The Scottish art of taking yourself half-seriously, and the single malt for it.
By Wolfburn Distillery
Thurso · Caithness
27 May 2026
9 min read

There is a black-and-white photograph in a certain pub in Cowdenbeath of Jim Baxter, taken in the summer of 1967, in which he is not doing very much. He is leaning on a wall outside Wembley, in a coat that fits him properly, smoking a cigarette as if he had invented it. The Scotland team has just beaten England 3-2. Baxter has just spent the closing minutes of the match doing keepie-uppies in the centre circle, in front of the World Cup holders, while ten of his teammates ran their lungs out behind him. He looks, in the photograph, like he is about to say something. He never quite gets round to it.

This is the spirit we are after.

· · ·

The thing about Baxter doing those keepie-uppies is not that he was disrespecting England, although it is sometimes told that way, and Baxter would not have argued. The thing about it is that he was disrespecting the match itself. He was, very gently and very deliberately, declaring that the result was not the point. Scotland were 3-2 up against the reigning world champions, and a sensible captain would have asked the team to see the thing out properly. Baxter was not a sensible captain. He was a Scotsman with the ball at his feet, with a minute on the clock that did not require him to do anything useful, and he chose to use that minute to make a small philosophical point at the expense of Bobby Charlton.

The point he was making was this. The result will be remembered for a while. The moment will be remembered for a very long time. If you can find a way to put the moment inside the result, you will have done your job. Baxter, that afternoon, put the moment inside the result. We have been telling the story ever since.

· · ·

Eleven years later, on a hot night in Mendoza in 1978, a small Aberdeenshire-trained midfielder named Archie Gemmill received the ball thirty yards from the Dutch goal in a World Cup group match, beat three defenders without appearing to try, and chipped the ball gently over the goalkeeper. There is a moment in the footage where Gemmill, having scored, half-turns to look at the bench, as if to check that this is really what has just happened. Then he runs into the corner with his arms out, in the way Scotsmen do when they have done something they quietly suspect they were not actually meant to be able to do.

Scotland went out of that tournament. We had been informed beforehand by our manager, a man called Ally MacLeod, that we were going to win it. We had been informed by Ally MacLeod that we were going to come home with the World Cup itself. There had been a homecoming parade scheduled at Hampden before a ball had been kicked. The parade happened anyway, because Scotland is the sort of country that has a homecoming parade for a team that has come home without winning the World Cup, and we will not apologise for it.

Gemmill's goal, however, is on the wall of every pub in this country that has a wall. Ally MacLeod's parade is on the wall of nowhere. The hedge has done its work. The moment has eaten the result.

· · ·

You can probably see where this is going.

Scotland are at the World Cup again. Scotland will, in all probability, lose to Brazil. Scotland may or may not lose to Haiti. Scotland may or may not lose to Morocco. The bookmakers have a view. We have a view. The view in this building is, broadly, mibbe aye, mibbe no, which is the view of every Scottish household this morning that is honest about it.

And so we have made a thing for the moments. Not for the results. The results will be what they are, and there will be no shortage of people willing to tell you whose fault each of them was. The moments will be ours. Some of them will be heartbroken. Some of them will be unexpectedly glorious. Some of them will look like a small midfielder running into a corner with his arms out, and some of them will look like Jim Baxter, calmly disrespecting the entire concept of a football match in the centre circle of Wembley.

The thing for the moments is sitting on the bench in Henderson Park as we type. It is a wooden box. Inside it are three bottles of single malt Scotch whisky, hand-bottled here in Thurso. Each one is for one of the three group-stage matches, and each one is dressed for the occasion. The 10 Year Old, in cream, for the opener against Haiti. The 8 Year Old, in cobalt, for the second against Morocco. The 12 Year Old, in gold, for the third against Brazil. Drams for the Tartan Army.

What you pour, you pour for the moment, not the result. That was Baxter's point in the centre circle in 1967, and Gemmill's point in the corner in Mendoza, and Ally MacLeod's point, in his way, at the homecoming parade for a team that had not won anything. The result will pass. The moment, if you hold it properly, will not.

We have made you something to hold the moment properly.

· · ·

The 10 Year Old goes in the Glencairn before kick-off on the Sunday. We have given it warm cream and gold because the opening fixture is an inherently optimistic act, and you do not open a tournament you do not think you are going to win, even if you are quietly certain you are not going to win it. Pour it before they walk out. Sit it on the table. Let it breathe.

The 8 Year Old goes on the table on the Friday, with the curtains half-drawn against the Foxborough light. The label is cobalt blue. The colour is cool because by the time we walk out for the second match, the first match has happened, and we know things we did not know before. The Morocco fixture will reward composure. Pour it slowly. Do not pour a second one before the seventieth minute.

The 12 Year Old goes wherever you are going to be on the Wednesday after midnight, when Scotland is playing Brazil and the country is somehow, against all reasonable expectation, holding its breath. The label is golden yellow. The whisky is the older brother of the set, with the deepest colour, and it gets the deepest moment. The Brazil match is the one we have all been thinking about since the draw. There is nothing to be gained from pretending otherwise. Pour it like you mean it.

The set comes in a wooden box. The kind a watchmaker might use to ship a serious thing. The interior is lined in a charcoal cloth that the Saltire would not be ashamed to be wrapped in. The three bottles sit in moulded recesses that hold them firmly enough that you could carry the box to Foxborough yourself, if you happened to be going.

We have set aside the first allocation for the newsletter list. The rest goes to the storefront on Monday. We are not going to make many. We will not be repeating the box, for the simple reason that we will not be repeating the summer.

· · ·

Pour them slowly. Take a beat before you taste them. Look at the bottle for a moment before you look at the score. Jim Baxter would have approved.

Mibbe aye, mibbe no. The bottle has been through worse than a back four.

Fortune favours the brave.
