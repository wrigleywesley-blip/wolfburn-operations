Wolfburn Journal · The launch dispatch

Three matches. Three drams. One summer.

A Wolfburn dispatch on the 2026 World Cup, the three single malts we have made for Scotland's three group matches, and the question every Scottish household is currently asking itself.
By Wolfburn Distillery
Thurso · Caithness
27 May 2026
9 min read

On Sunday 14 June, at the inhumane hour of nine o'clock in the evening BST, Scotland will walk out at Gillette Stadium in Foxborough, Massachusetts, and play a World Cup match for the first time since the millennium was a different shape. The team has waited twenty-eight years for the sentence. The country has waited twenty-eight years for the sentence. Most of the players in our colours that evening will have spent most of their adult lives preparing for the sentence. The sentence does not, at present, contain a result. We will keep you informed.

What we have done about it sits in a wooden box on the bench in front of us. Three bottles of single malt Scotch whisky, hand-bottled here in Thurso, released as a single Wolfburn set to mark Scotland's three group-stage fixtures at the 2026 World Cup. The 10 Year Old, in warm cream and gold, for the opener against Haiti on Sunday 14 June at Foxborough. The 8 Year Old, in cobalt blue, for the second match against Morocco on Friday 19 June, also at Foxborough. The 12 Year Old, in golden yellow, for the third match against Brazil on Wednesday 24 June, in Miami, at the unhelpful hour of two o'clock in the morning local-to-us. Drams for the Tartan Army, is what we have called them.

The set comes as a single release. You buy the box, you receive three bottles, you open them in the order the matches arrive, and you pour them whichever way the result goes. The bottles do not require a particular outcome to justify themselves. They have been here longer than the result. They will be here when the result has stopped mattering, which in our experience tends to happen by about the following Wednesday.

· · ·

The reason there are three bottles, in three different colours, in three different ages, is not because we wanted to overcomplicate the gift section of the website. It is because each match is a different kind of evening, and a Scottish whisky person knows you do not pour the same dram for an opener against a team you are expected to beat as for a closer against a team that has won the tournament five times.

The 10 Year Old, in cream and gold, is the opening-match dram. Openings are an inherently optimistic act. You do not begin a tournament you do not believe you can win, even if you are quietly certain you will not win it. The 10 Year Old is matured in second-fill Oloroso sherry hogsheads and ex-bourbon casks, which gives it a depth of character with no inclination to start with a speech. Pour it before the players come out. Sit it on the table. Look at it once. Get on with the match.

The 8 Year Old, in cobalt blue, is the second-match dram. By the time we walk out for Morocco on the Friday, the Haiti match will have happened, and we will know things we did not know on Wednesday. Whichever way the first result went, the second match is now the match that matters, because every group has a match in the middle that decides what happens in the third, and we are not exempt from arithmetic. The cobalt colour is cooler because that is what the moment asks for. Pour this one slowly. The 8 Year Old is the youngest in the set, brighter on the nose, and it will reward you for not rushing it.

The 12 Year Old, in golden yellow, is the Brazil dram. Two things to know about the Brazil match. The first is that the kick-off in Miami is at two o'clock in the morning BST, which means most of the country will either be awake when they should be asleep or asleep when they should be awake, and either choice is correct. The second is that Brazil have won the World Cup five times. Scotland have not. We can sit with that. The 12 Year Old is the flagship of the Wolfburn range, twelve years in oak in a building on the very top edge of the country, and it gets the deepest moment of the three. Whether the Wednesday morning turns out to be a famous Scottish win or a famous Scottish defeat, the colour will be correct. Pour it like you mean it.

· · ·

You will notice immediately that we have not committed to a particular outcome. The bottles do not say "to celebrate." They do not say "to commiserate." They say "Scotland v Haiti." They say "Scotland v Morocco." They say "Scotland v Brazil." What you do with them depends, in the end, on what the eleven players in our colours do with the ninety minutes in front of them.

This is not cowardice. This is what we know.

Scotland's football history is not a history of results. It is a history of moments. There is a passage in any Scottish writer worth reading about Jim Baxter at Wembley in 1967, where Baxter, having helped put Scotland 3-2 up against the World Cup holders, spent the closing minutes of the match doing keepie-uppies in the centre circle. The English players, by all accounts, did not find it funny. Baxter did not stop. Scotland won 3-2. The keepie-uppies are remembered. The result, less so.

Eleven years later, on a hot night in Mendoza in 1978, a small Aberdeenshire-trained midfielder named Archie Gemmill received the ball thirty yards from the Dutch goal in a World Cup group match, beat three defenders without appearing to try, and chipped the ball gently over the goalkeeper. There is a moment in the footage where Gemmill, having scored, half-turns to look at the bench, as if to check that this is really what has just happened. Then he runs into the corner with his arms out. Scotland went out of that tournament. The goal is on the wall of every pub in this country that has a wall. The exit is on the wall of nowhere. The moment has eaten the result.

This is the trick of the thing. We are taught from a very young age that the result is a small part of the story, and the small part of the story is the part we will probably get wrong. The rest is ours.

So we have made the whisky for the rest.

· · ·

The wooden box is the kind a watchmaker might use to ship a serious thing. Lined in a charcoal cloth that the Saltire would not be ashamed to be wrapped in. The three bottles sit in moulded recesses that hold them firmly enough that you could carry the box to Foxborough yourself, if you happened to be going. Some of you are. Most of you are not. The box does not mind.

First allocation to the newsletter list. Storefront goes live on Monday. We are not going to make many. We will not be repeating the set, for the simple reason that we will not be repeating the summer. Twenty-eight years was the wait. Three matches is the moment. Three bottles is the answer.

Sunday the 14th. Friday the 19th. Wednesday the 24th. Pour them slowly. Look at the bottle for a moment before you look at the score. Mibbe aye, mibbe no, you will have an excellent summer either way.

Fortune favours the brave.

03 · Match-day blog series

Three short dispatches, one before each fixture.

Each lands on the morning of the match (or the night before for Brazil, given the kick-off time). 400-500 words each. Template-driven so we ship them inside two hours of confirming the starting eleven. "Welcome to Caithness, [opponent]" is the running title format.

MATCH 1 · 14 JUNE · 16:00 BST

Welcome to Caithness, Haiti.

Tonight, Sunday 14 June, at nine o'clock in the evening, Scotland plays a World Cup match for the first time since 1998. We have prepared the whisky. You bring the optimism.

We were thinking about you, Haiti, on the way to work this morning. It is, by all accounts, an unusual feeling to be a country in the middle of a Highland summer and to be thinking about a country in the middle of the Caribbean at the same time, but here we are, and there you are, and in five hours and forty-three minutes the eleven of you will walk out at Gillette Stadium and play the eleven of us at a World Cup. Stranger things have happened. Almost none of them in Foxborough.

The Wolfburn 10 Year Old is on the table next to the typewriter. We will not be drinking it yet. We will be drinking it shortly before kick-off, which by our reckoning is twenty minutes from now in your kitchen, an hour from now in the Edinburgh house Scotland's manager is currently driving through, and however long it takes the Foxborough tannoy to find the words "and now, the national anthems."

The 10 Year Old, the cream-and-gold one, is the opener of the three. We have given it the warm cream because openings are an inherently optimistic act, and we did not want to dress the first dram of the summer in anything that looked as if it knew the score in advance. It is matured in second-fill Oloroso sherry hogsheads and ex-bourbon casks, has the depth of character of a whisky that has been here a while, and the temperament of a whisky that does not feel the need to introduce itself loudly. It will be in the Glencairn before the kit comes out.

We do not know the result, Haiti. We know you have a goalkeeper who has been beating people who paid a lot of money to bet against him, and a midfield that runs at people who paid a lot of money to defend against them. We know we have an Andy Robertson and a Scott McTominay and a country that has spent twenty-eight years wondering whether either of them would ever wear the dark blue at a World Cup. We know we are going to find out by quarter to eleven this evening.

Either way, we have prepared the whisky. The cream bottle is for the opener. Mibbe aye, mibbe no.

Fortune favours the brave.
Wolfburn Distillery · Thurso · five hours forty-three minutes to kick-off
MATCH 2 · 19 JUNE · 15:00 BST

Welcome to Caithness, Morocco.

Tonight, Friday 19 June, at eight o'clock in the evening, Scotland plays its second match of the tournament. The cobalt bottle comes off the shelf.

The first match has happened, Morocco, and we have not slept the same way since. We will not tell you what the result was, although you can probably find out from the television in the lobby, because telling you what the result was would lend it more authority than it currently deserves. The result was a hundred-and-twenty-minute kind of result. The dram that followed was a sit-by-the-window kind of dram. We have moved on.

The Wolfburn 8 Year Old is on the table. The cobalt-blue bottle, the youngest of the three, the cooler colour of the set. We have given it cobalt because by the time we walk out to play you, Morocco, the country has had five days to think about what it saw on Sunday, and a cooler colour is what those five days call for. The 8 Year Old is brighter on the nose than the 10 Year Old, more direct on the palate, and entirely uninterested in the homework Scotland has done on your wing-backs.

Things we know about you, Morocco. You play with a discipline that the rest of the group is not going to be able to copy. You beat Spain in 2022. You finished above Belgium and Croatia. You are not, in any sensible reading of the form guide, the second-easiest match of our group. We have looked at the form guide twice this week and put it back in the drawer, because looking at the form guide does not seem to be having the effect on our nerves that we were hoping for.

What we do know is the dram. The cobalt bottle, the 8 Year Old, the second pour. Pour it slowly. The composure of the second match is the composure of a country that has been here before, and we have not been here before, but the bottle has, in its way, and it can hold the composure for us.

Tonight, eight o'clock BST. Foxborough again. Mibbe aye, mibbe no.

Fortune favours the brave.

MATCH 3 · 23 JUNE · 20:00 BST (eve of fixture)

Welcome to Caithness, Brazil.

Tonight, the night between Tuesday and Wednesday, at two o'clock in the morning BST, Scotland plays Brazil at a World Cup. The 12 Year Old has been on the table since six this evening.

There is no graceful way to write to you, Brazil. The five stars on your shirt are not a rhetorical decoration. The eleven of you that will run out at the Hard Rock Stadium tomorrow morning will, between them, be worth more money than the entire Scottish football pyramid, and the goalkeeper who Scotland has to beat tomorrow morning to take a point off you is, by general consent, the best goalkeeper in the world. We have not pretended otherwise this week. We are not about to start now.

We have made the dram correspondingly serious. The Wolfburn 12 Year Old, in golden yellow, is the flagship of our range. Twelve years in oak. Hand-selected Oloroso sherry butts and ex-bourbon casks. Forty-six percent vol. Non-chill filtered. Natural colour, which on a 12 Year Old means the colour you are looking at in the Glencairn is the colour the whisky chose for itself in the cask, and not the colour someone in marketing thought might play better on a shelf. We have given it gold because what the moment asks for is gold, regardless of which way the result goes.

You will excuse us, Brazil, for spending most of this letter talking about the whisky and not about the match. We have rehearsed this match in our heads since the draw was made. We have rehearsed it in three or four different outcomes, most of which involve a moment of unexpected joy followed by a passage of sustained suffering. We have rehearsed it as a famous Scottish defeat with our heads held high. We have rehearsed it as a famous Scottish goal nobody scored against you. We have rehearsed it as Archie Gemmill all over again, except this time in a stadium with air conditioning. The rehearsal has not, frankly, made it any easier.

What we know is the dram. The gold bottle. The 12 Year Old. Pour it slowly, somewhere between half-past one and a quarter to two in the morning UK time. Sit it on the side table. Look at it once. Do not, under any circumstances, look at the form guide.

By half past three in the morning we will know what the summer of 2026 was. By eight o'clock the same morning we will be on the way to work. The country has been here before, in a manner of speaking. The bottle has been here longer. Mibbe aye, mibbe no.

Fortune favours the brave.
