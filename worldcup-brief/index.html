01 · Executive summary

The campaign, in thirty seconds.

Scotland turns up to the World Cup. Wolfburn turns up alongside, with the right line for every result. The tagline already exists. We're just walking into it.

  • Anchor phrase · "Mibbe aye, mibbe no" — the Tartan Army's national hedge. Dry, knowing, ours.
  • Hook · Fortune favours the brave. The existing Wolfburn tagline becomes the existing campaign tagline. No re-platform required.
  • Voice · editorial, dry-humoured, place-led, considered. Cheeky on the surface, quietly upper-class underneath. Never lad-mag, never breathless.

02 · Strategic context

Why the football summer matters for Wolfburn.

The 2026 FIFA World Cup runs 11 June to 19 July across the United States, Canada, and Mexico. Scotland are competing. For the team to confirm: group, fixtures, kick-off times in BST.

For Wolfburn, this is the single most concentrated marketing moment of the year. Three things make it specifically Wolfburn's window:

1 · The tagline pre-exists the campaign

Fortune favours the brave is not a slogan we are inventing for the World Cup. It is the Wolfburn line. Scotland's whole footballing identity is the underdog turning up — bravery without certainty. The brand and the moment are already aligned. We are not selling a story, we are pointing at one.

2 · The audience is already there

Storefront analytics show our most engaged geographies are Scottish (Glasgow, broader UK), French (Paris is the #1 source city), and US (Council Bluffs Iowa, City of London, suburban). Every one of these audiences will be watching the World Cup in some form. Email and blog go where they already are.

3 · POS strength tells us where the brand really lives

Recent admin data has the distillery shop running at £14.5K vs £8.2K online over the May 1-8 period. People come to Caithness for the brand, not the other way around. The campaign's editorial register has to honour that — we are a destination distillery, not a discount-led DTC label. The blog is the closest thing to an in-person dram for people who can't make the journey north.

What we are not doing

  • No World Cup branding, no FIFA marks. Avoids licensing exposure and frankly suits us better — we don't need their crest.
  • No price-led messaging, no "20% off match day" calls. Off-brand and unnecessary.
  • No bombast. No "Come on Scotland!" all-caps energy. Scotland's whole charm is the quiet shrug. We meet that.
  • No betting partnerships, no influencer plays, no patriotic shouting.

The Highland gentleman

Older Scottish, late 50s through mid-70s. Reads with a glass already poured. Knows the difference between a 10 year old and a 12 year old without prompting. Doesn't need to be told that Scotland will probably lose — he's been Scottish all his life. Wants the writing to share his weather-worn humour, not deny it. Will forgive almost any wit and almost no cliche. Reads on a desktop, opens emails on a Sunday morning.

The Scotland-curious international

Late 30s to 60s. Parisian, German, Iowa transplant, London-Scot. Buys whisky as a personal pleasure, often as gifts. Has been to Edinburgh or wants to. Reads English fluently but appreciates being walked through the Scottish references — that's part of the charm of subscribing to a brand like ours. We give them a guide and a wink at the same time.

What both readers want from this campaign

  • A reason to feel they're in on something. The blog is a "fellow traveller" register, not a sales register.
  • Permission to enjoy the football tournament without surrendering taste. Wolfburn is the way you stay civilised through a sporting summer.
  • A dram for the result — whatever it turns out to be.
04 · Anchor thesis

"Mibbe aye, mibbe no."

If you ask a Tartan Army veteran on the morning of a fixture whether Scotland will win today, the most reliable answer you'll receive is some form of "aye, mibbe aye, mibbe no." It is the national hedge. It is also the perfect Wolfburn voice.

The phrase says: I am not naive enough to expect a win, but I am stubborn enough to show up anyway. I have a glass in my hand. I will not be moved. — the thesis of the campaign

It threads three things at once, which is what makes it usable across the whole summer:

  • It is Scottish to the bone. No translation required for the audience that matters.
  • It is dry without being mean. It mocks no one. It just hedges. That is the Wolfburn tonal sweet spot.
  • It rhymes with the brand. "Fortune favours the brave" is the brave answer. "Mibbe aye, mibbe no" is the honest answer. Together they are the campaign in three words and four.

The campaign uses "mibbe aye, mibbe no" as the running phrase — subject lines, opening sentences, sign-offs, the email subject for the launch — and "Fortune favours the brave" as the resolution. The reader earns the second by sitting through the first.

05 · Voice quick-reference

How to write like Wolfburn.

Pin this card next to the keyboard. Everything in this campaign should pass these checks before it ships. Voice (the brand QA agent) reviews every output against this list.

DO
Speak with quiet confidence. The whisky has a 10-year-old, a 12-year-old, and a tagline. Nothing else needs to shout.
DO
Lead with the place. Thurso, Caithness, the Pentland Firth. Place is the brand and the brand is the place.
DO
Write the way an older Scot tells a story — sparingly, with timing, with a beat before the punchline.
DO
Use "single malt Scotch whisky". Never "scotch". The American spelling is fine in dialogue or quotation, never in our voice.
DO
Be cheeky about the moment. Be never cheeky about the audience. There is a line between dry humour and condescension; we stay on the correct side of it.
DO
Earn the punchline. Set the scene first. The Scottish ear hears the joke coming and rewards the patience.
DON'T
Use the words smooth, approachable, easy drinker, perfect for. Cliche tasting-note vocabulary. They are immediate red flags to a literate audience.
DON'T
Lean on price, discount, sale, limited time, save. We don't compete on price and we won't pretend to.
DON'T
Use em-dashes in any draft (Wesley's preference). Use commas, colons, full stops, en-dashes. Mid-sentence pauses are achievable without them.
DON'T
Shout. No exclamation marks unless someone is genuinely surprised inside the writing. "Come on Scotland!" is for the pub, not for the dispatch.
DON'T
Punch down at any opponent country. We tease, we don't insult. Imagine the post being read by a Norwegian fan over their breakfast. Would they smile?
DON'T
Use FIFA, World Cup, or any competition marks unless legal has cleared them. "The summer tournament", "the championship", "the football summer" are our workarounds.

10 · Sample copy

One dispatch, in full, to set the tone.

If the rest of the brief is convincing on paper, this is what it sounds like in practice. The cornerstone piece, drafted in full. Voice, length, sign-off, all on-pattern. The team can use this as the template for tone calibration on every other post.

Dispatch · 01

Mibbe aye, mibbe no.

A short defence of the Scottish shrug, ahead of a long summer.

Ask a man in Thurso whether the team will win on Saturday and you will receive a sentence that takes him three minutes to finish. He will begin with "aye", he will not end with "aye", and there will be at least one stretch of silence in which you are invited to look out of the window and admire the weather. This is not evasion. This is, in fact, the most precise answer in the world, delivered in the only language that knows how to say it.

The phrase, eventually, will be some version of "mibbe aye, mibbe no." It is the national hedge. It is also, on closer inspection, the national religion. Hope, tempered by experience. Optimism, kept on the shelf with the good glasses. Scotland turning up to a football tournament with hand luggage and low expectations is not pessimism. It is good manners.

You can drink whisky to that. You can drink whisky to a great many things, but you can drink whisky to that particularly well. The bottle has been through worse than a back four. The bottle has seen a Pentland Firth winter and not flinched. If anyone in this house is going to lose composure during a penalty shoot-out, it will not be the whisky.

So this summer, we are putting out a series of short dispatches under the title Mibbe aye, mibbe no. Some of them will be about the football, in the sense that the football is the thing that everyone in Scotland is currently pretending not to be too excited about. Some of them will be about Caithness, which is where the whisky is from and where some of us are watching the games. Some of them will be about whatever a Scottish summer does to a person who has lived through enough of them to know better than to expect anything.

The first thing we are sending out alongside the dispatches is a new single malt Scotch whisky we have made for the occasion. We will tell you about it properly in a separate note. The short version is that it is a dram for whichever way the results go, which is to say, a dram for the most likely outcome. It is bottled in measured quantities. It does not require a result to justify it.

We have not put a football crest anywhere on the bottle. We did not need to. A bottle of Wolfburn already carries the only crest that earns its place — "fortune favours the brave", which is what we think when we watch Scotland line up against teams who do this for a living, and what we think when we ourselves consider whether the year ahead will be kind to a small distillery on the very top edge of the country.

If it is kind, mibbe we will say so. If it is not, mibbe we will still say so, with the right kind of pause beforehand. Either way, the pour is the same.

The dispatches will be brief. The whisky is not. We will be here all summer.

Fortune favours the brave.

Total: 524 words. Light editing welcome. The first sentence sets a register the team should hold across every other post. The sign-off line is a contract: every dispatch ends this way, no exceptions.

13 · Research notes · sources for the writer

Where to dig deeper.

For the writer producing the posts. Tone calibration, fact-checking, vocabulary, and atmosphere. Not citations — references.

Scottish dry humour, in print

  • Ian Rankin, the Rebus novels. The way an Edinburgh detective says exactly what he means and the page somehow gets it wrong.
  • James Robertson, The Testament of Gideon Mack. Highland register at full strength.
  • Anything by Janice Galloway, for sentence rhythm.
  • Limmy's Daft Wee Stories, for permission to be brief.

Tartan Army register

  • Archive footage of the 1998 World Cup Scotland coverage. The way the commentary held composure through the heartbreak. That is the tonal target.
  • The Tartan Army Magazine archives, for actual fan vocabulary in print.
  • Any of Stuart Cosgrove's football writing.

Whisky writing we can stand behind

  • Iain Banks, Raw Spirit. The reference text for tonal mood when writing about Scotch.
  • Dave Broom, anything. For accuracy without pretension.
  • The Spectator's wine column. For how to be cheeky without surrendering taste.

Caithness, place

  • Neil Gunn, Highland River. The water source of all of this.
  • Wolfburn's existing distillery diary entries, for in-house tonal continuity.
  • The North Coast 500 marketing materials, only as a counter-example. Wolfburn does not need to sell the road.

Vocabulary worth knowing for the international audience

fashed (annoyed) · scunnered (fed up, in a particularly Scottish way) · minted (well-funded) · peely-wally (pale) · Hampden roar (the noise the national stadium makes when Scotland score) · thrawn (stubborn, but with a hint of dignity) · blether (a long chat) · haud yer wheesht (be quiet) · nae bother (no problem, often more sincere than it sounds) · aye right (no, almost always meaning no, despite appearances).

Things the writer should fact-check before publish

  • All fixture details. We do not invent kick-off times.
  • Manager name and tenure, before dispatch 07.
  • Any specific quote attributed to a real person. If unsure, paraphrase or remove.
  • Any reference to "the World Cup" by name, in case the team's policy is to use a workaround for licensing safety. Default phrases: "the summer tournament", "the championship", "the football summer".
