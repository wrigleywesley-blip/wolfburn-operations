Dispatch · 01 · The hedge

Mibbe aye, mibbe no.

A Wolfburn dispatch for the summer of 2026, and a wee box of single malt for whichever way it goes.

If you ask a man on Thurso high street this morning whether Scotland will get out of their group, you will receive a sentence with a beginning, a middle, and an end, and somewhere between those three points you will lose track of which answer is being given. He will start by saying "aye." He will not finish by saying "aye." There will be a long pause in the middle in which the weather will be invited to participate. By the time he has finished the sentence, you and he will be friends, and you will have no idea what he thinks about Scotland's chances of making the round of sixteen.

This is not vagueness. This is the most precise answer in the world, delivered in the only language that knows how to say it. The phrase, when it eventually arrives, will be some version of mibbe aye, mibbe no. It is the national hedge. It is also, on closer inspection, the national religion. Hope tempered by experience. Optimism kept on the shelf with the good glasses. Scotland turning up to a football tournament with hand luggage and low expectations is not pessimism. It is good manners.

This summer, we are going to test that arrangement.

· · ·

Twenty-eight years is a long time to wait for the sentence we have finally allowed ourselves to write. Scotland are at the World Cup. The last time we managed it, the world was a wider place, most of you reading this were not yet at school, and a small distillery on the very top edge of the country had not yet opened its doors. The men who are about to walk out in Foxborough and Miami in our colours have been preparing for this their whole adult lives. Most of them have not yet started getting nervous, which is the most Scottish quality of all.

We have made you a thing.

It is sitting in a wooden box on the bench in front of us as we type. Three bottles of single malt Scotch whisky, hand-bottled in Thurso, released as a single set to mark the three group-stage fixtures. Aged 10 Years, cream and gold, for the opening match against Haiti on Sunday 14 June. Aged 8 Years, cobalt blue, for the second against Morocco on Friday 19 June. Aged 12 Years, golden yellow, for the third against Brazil on Wednesday 24 June. Drams for the Tartan Army, is what we have called them.

You will notice immediately that we have not committed to a particular outcome. The bottles do not say "to celebrate," and they do not say "to commiserate." They say "Scotland v Haiti." They say "Scotland v Morocco." They say "Scotland v Brazil." What you do with them depends, in the end, on what the eleven players in our colours do with the ninety minutes in front of them.

This is not cowardice. This is what we know.

· · ·

There is a passage in any Scottish writer worth reading about Jim Baxter at Wembley in 1967. Scotland had gone 2-1 up against an England team that had won the World Cup the year before, and Baxter, instead of helping the team see the match out, started doing keepie-uppies in his own half. The English players, by all accounts, did not find it funny. Baxter did not stop. Scotland won 3-2. The keepie-uppies are remembered. The result, less so.

Archie Gemmill scored that goal against Holland in 1978 that you have seen on a wall in a pub somewhere. He beat three defenders to do it. Scotland went out of the group anyway, having been informed by their manager beforehand that they were going to win the tournament. The goal is remembered. The exit, less so.

This is the trick of the thing. Scotland's football history is not a history of results. It is a history of moments. The hedge is in the moments. We are taught from a very young age that the result is a small part of the story, and the small part of the story is the part we will probably get wrong. The rest is ours.

So we have made the whisky for the rest.

· · ·

The cream one comes first. We have aged it ten years. We have given it the warm cream and gold dress because the first match is the opening match, and openings are an inherently optimistic act. You do not open a tournament you do not think you are going to win, even if you are quietly certain you are not going to win it. Pour this one before kick-off on the Sunday. Allow it room.

The cobalt blue comes second. We have aged it eight years. The colour is cooler, because by the time we walk out for the second match, the first match has happened, and we know things we did not know before. Whatever the first result was, the second match is now the match that matters. Pour this one slowly. The Morocco fixture will reward composure.

The golden yellow comes third. We have aged it twelve years. It is the older brother of the three, with the deepest colour, and it gets the deepest moment. The Brazil match is the one we have all been thinking about since the draw. There is nothing to be gained from pretending otherwise. We have given it gold because gold is what the moment asks for, whether the moment turns out to be a famous win or a famous defeat. Pour it like you mean it.

· · ·

The box is wooden. The kind a watchmaker might use to ship a serious thing. The interior is lined in a charcoal cloth that the Saltire would not be ashamed to be wrapped in. The three bottles sit in moulded recesses that hold them firmly enough that you could carry the box to Foxborough yourself, if you happened to be going.

We have set aside the first allocation for the newsletter list. The rest goes to the storefront on Monday. We are not going to make many. We will not be repeating the box, for the simple reason that we will not be repeating the summer.

What we do know is what is in the box. Three single malts. Three matches. One small wooden release of Scotland, in glass.

The match-day dram is not a recipe. It is a ritual. It is what you reach for when there is nothing else to be said, and the country has not stopped saying things for the last twenty-eight years. Some quiet, this June, will be welcome.

Mibbe aye, mibbe no. We are here either way.

Fortune favours the brave.

Dispatch · 02 · The box

The wooden box, opened.

What each bottle is for. In order. Without spoiling the surprise.

The wooden box arrived on the bench at Henderson Park on a Monday morning in early May. It had come up the road from a small workshop in Aberdeenshire that has been making serious wooden boxes since long before anybody in this building was thinking about a World Cup. Inside it, when we opened it, were three bottles, lying neatly in moulded recesses, and a small printed card explaining what each of them was for. The printed card was important. The bottles, taken on their own, would not have told you the whole story.

This is the story.

· · ·

The first bottle is the 10 Year Old. The label is cream, the foil is gold, and the date on the bottom reads Sunday 14 June 2026 · Foxborough USA. This is the opening match, Scotland v Haiti, and we have dressed the bottle for it. The cream is warm in the way an early-summer morning is warm — full of promise, slightly unspoken about it. The 10 Year Old itself is a Highland single malt matured in second-fill Oloroso sherry hogsheads and ex-bourbon casks, which is to say it is a whisky with depth of character but no inclination to start with a speech.

The pour for this one is before kick-off. Pour it into a Glencairn, sit it on the table, look at it once before the players come out, and leave it. If we win, you will get back to it in due course. If we draw, you will get back to it more quickly than that. If we lose, the dram will still be there, and the bottle will not remind you of anything painful, which is more than the highlights package will manage.

· · ·

The second bottle is the 8 Year Old. The label is cobalt blue, the foil is gold, and the date reads Friday 19 June 2026 · Foxborough USA. This is the Morocco match, and the cobalt is doing two jobs. It is the cool, sober colour of a country that has had five days to think about the first result. It is also a small nod to the Atlas Mountains, without committing to anything as gauche as a national costume.

The 8 Year Old is the youngest in the set, and the youngest in the Wolfburn core range. It has a touch more brightness on the nose than the 10 Year Old, which suits the second match in a tournament, when the body is starting to remember that this is going to go on for a while. Pour it after dinner. Take it into the room you watch matches in. Do not pour a second one before the seventieth minute.

· · ·

The third bottle is the 12 Year Old. The label is golden yellow, the foil is gold, and the date reads Wednesday 24 June 2026 · Miami USA. This is the Brazil match, and there is a longer conversation to be had about why we have given the oldest whisky to the third match. The short version is that the Brazil fixture is the match we have all been thinking about since the draw. It is the match every Scotland supporter has rehearsed in their head, in various wildly differing outcomes. It is the match the entire group stage points at, regardless of how we have got there. It deserves the seriousness of the 12 Year Old, in the same way that a serious meeting deserves a good coat.

The 12 Year Old is the flagship. It is matured in hand-selected Oloroso sherry butts and ex-bourbon casks, and the result is a whisky with a lingering finish that does not require a particular result to justify itself. We have given it gold because gold is what the moment asks for. Whether the moment turns out to be a famous win or a famous defeat, the colour is correct.

· · ·

The set comes in the wooden box. The box is lined in charcoal cloth. The whole thing has the weight of an object you might keep on a shelf afterwards, regardless of what happens to the bottles. We have not numbered the editions, because the set itself is the edition. When the box is gone, the box is gone. We will not repeat it, for the simple reason that we will not repeat the summer.

Three bottles. Three matches. One small wooden release of Scotland, in glass.

Mibbe aye, mibbe no.

Blog 3 · Heritage

"What Jim Baxter knew"

~1,300 words. The heritage long-read. For the third-week newsletter and shareable across the Scottish football press. Goes deep on the 1967 Wembley moment, Archie Gemmill's 1978 goal, and Ally MacLeod's hubris. Ties the whisky in the back third.

Dispatch · 03 · Heritage

What Jim Baxter knew.

On Wembley, 1967. On Mendoza, 1978. On a wooden box, 2026. The Scottish art of taking yourself half-seriously, and the whisky for it.

There is a black-and-white photograph in a particular pub in Cowdenbeath of Jim Baxter, taken in the summer of 1967, in which he is not doing very much. He is leaning on a wall outside Wembley, in a coat that fits him properly, smoking a cigarette as if he had invented it. The Scotland team has just beaten England 3-2. Baxter has just spent the closing minutes of the match doing keepie-uppies in the centre circle, in front of the world champions, while ten of his teammates ran their lungs out behind him. He looks, in the photograph, like he is about to say something. He never quite gets round to it.

This is the spirit we are after.

· · ·

The thing about Baxter doing those keepie-uppies is not that he was disrespecting England, although it is sometimes told that way, and Baxter would not have argued. The thing about it is that he was disrespecting the match. He was, very gently and very deliberately, declaring that the result was not the point. Scotland were 3-2 up against the reigning World Cup holders, and a sensible captain would have asked the team to see the thing out properly. Baxter was not a sensible captain. He was a Scotsman with the ball at his feet, with a minute on the clock that did not require him to do anything useful, and he chose to use that minute to make a small philosophical point at the expense of Bobby Charlton.

The point he was making was this. The result will be remembered for a while. The moment will be remembered for a very long time. If you can find a way to put the moment inside the result, you will have done your job. Baxter, that afternoon, put the moment inside the result. We have been telling the story ever since.

· · ·

Eleven years later, on a hot night in Mendoza in 1978, a small Aberdeenshire-trained midfielder named Archie Gemmill received the ball thirty yards from the Dutch goal in a World Cup group game, beat three defenders without appearing to try, and chipped the ball gently over the goalkeeper. There is a moment in the footage where Gemmill, having scored, half-turns to look at the bench, as if to check that this is really what has just happened. Then he runs into the corner with his arms out, in the way Scotsmen do when they have done something they suspect they were not actually meant to be able to do.

Scotland went out of that tournament. We had been informed beforehand by our manager, a man called Ally MacLeod, that we were going to win it. We had been informed by Ally MacLeod that we were going to come home with the World Cup itself. There had been a homecoming parade scheduled at Hampden before a ball had been kicked. The parade happened anyway, because Scotland is the sort of country that has a homecoming parade for a team that has come home without winning the World Cup, and we will not apologise for it.

Gemmill's goal, however, is on the wall of every pub in this country that has a wall. Ally MacLeod's parade is on the wall of nowhere. The hedge has done its work. The moment has eaten the result.

· · ·

You can probably see where this is going.

Scotland are at the World Cup again. Scotland will, in all probability, lose to Brazil. Scotland may or may not lose to Haiti. Scotland may or may not lose to Morocco. The bookmakers have a view. We have a view. The view in this building is, broadly, mibbe aye, mibbe no, which is the view of every Scottish household this morning that is honest about it.

And so we have made a thing for the moments. Not for the results. The results will be what they are, and there will be no shortage of people willing to tell you whose fault each of them was. The moments will be ours. Some of them will be heartbroken. Some of them will be unexpectedly glorious. Some of them will look like a small midfielder running into a corner with his arms out, and some of them will look like Jim Baxter, calmly disrespecting the entire concept of a football match in the centre circle of Wembley.

The thing for the moments is sitting on the bench in Henderson Park as we type. It is a wooden box. Inside it are three bottles of single malt Scotch whisky. Each one is for one of the three group-stage matches, and each one is dressed for the occasion. The 10 Year Old, in cream, for the opener. The 8 Year Old, in cobalt, for the second. The 12 Year Old, in gold, for the third. Drams for the Tartan Army.

What you pour, you pour for the moment, not the result. That was Baxter's point in the centre circle in 1967, and Gemmill's point in the corner in Mendoza, and Ally MacLeod's point, in his way, at the homecoming parade for a team that had not won anything. The result will pass. The moment, if you hold it properly, will not.

We have made you something to hold the moment properly.

· · ·

The 10 Year Old goes in the Glencairn before kick-off on the Sunday. The 8 Year Old goes on the table on the Friday, with the curtains half-drawn against the Foxborough light. The 12 Year Old goes wherever you are going to be on the Wednesday after midnight, when Scotland is playing Brazil and the country is somehow, against all reasonable expectation, holding its breath.

Pour them slowly. Take a beat before you taste them. Look at the bottle for a moment before you look at the score. Jim Baxter would have approved.

Mibbe aye, mibbe no. The bottle has been through worse than a back four.

Fortune favours the brave.
